Module 1 · Fluid Mechanics

Fluid mechanics studies liquids and gases. This module focuses on fluids at rest: density, pressure, hydrostatic pressure, Pascal's principle, and Archimedes' principle.

1. Fluid basics and volume

A fluid flows and takes the shape of its container. Liquids have a free surface and are nearly incompressible; gases expand to fill the container and are more compressible.

Volume conversions
\[1\,\mathrm{m^3}=10^6\,\mathrm{cm^3}=1000\,\mathrm{L}\]
Because \(1\,\mathrm{m}=100\,\mathrm{cm}\), cube the length conversion.
Mass density
\[\rho=\frac{m}{V}\qquad m=\rho V\]
SI unit: \(\mathrm{kg/m^3}\).

Specific gravity and average density

\[\rho_{avg}=\frac{m_{total}}{V_{total}}\qquad SG=\frac{\rho_{object}}{\rho_{water}}\]

Specific gravity has no unit. For hollow objects, use the total outside volume, including the trapped-air space, when finding average density.

2. Pressure and hydrostatic pressure

\[p=\frac{F_\perp}{A}\qquad 1\,\mathrm{Pa}=1\,\mathrm{N/m^2}\]

Pressure is force perpendicular to a surface divided by area. The same force on a smaller area produces greater pressure. At a point in a fluid at rest, pressure acts in all directions.

\[p=p_0+\rho gd\qquad p_{gauge}=p-p_{atm}\]

Depth (d) is measured vertically from the liquid surface. If the surface is open to the atmosphere, \(p_{gauge}=\rho gd\). Absolute pressure includes the surface pressure (p_0).

Worked example · submarine at 300 m

Use seawater \(\rho=1030\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(p_0=101300\,\mathrm{Pa}\), and \(g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

\[p=101300+(1030)(9.8)(300)\approx3.13\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\]

Divide by \(101300\,\mathrm{Pa/atm}\): \(p\approx30.9\,\mathrm{atm}\). Gauge pressure is about \(3.03\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\).

3. Pascal's principle and hydraulic lifts

A pressure change applied to a confined, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished throughout the fluid. In an ideal lift at the same elevation:

\[\frac{F_1}{A_1}=\frac{F_2}{A_2}\qquad A_1d_1=A_2d_2\]

Hydraulics gain force but pay with distance. For circular pistons \(A=\pi r^2\), so force follows the square of the diameter ratio.

Worked example · 5 cm piston, 25 cm piston, 100 N
\[F_2=F_1\frac{A_2}{A_1}=100\left(\frac{25}{5}\right)^2=2500\,\mathrm{N}\]

Do not use \(25/5=5\) as the force ratio; area scales with diameter squared.

4. Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle

Pressure is greater at the bottom of a submerged object than at the top, creating a net upward force. Archimedes' principle says buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid.

\[F_B=\rho_{fluid}V_{displaced}g\qquad W_{apparent}=W-F_B\]

Use the density of the fluid and the displaced fluid volume, not automatically the object's density or full volume. For a fully submerged object (V_{displaced}=V_{object}); for a partially submerged object, use only the submerged portion.

Sink\(\rho_{object}>\rho_{fluid}\)
Float\(\rho_{object}<\rho_{fluid}\)
Neutral\(\rho_{object}=\rho_{fluid}\)
\[\frac{V_{sub}}{V_{object}}=\frac{\rho_{object}}{\rho_{fluid}}\quad\text{for free floating equilibrium}\]
Worked example · submerged block

A block has \(V=0.020\,\mathrm{m^3}\) in water, \(\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\).

\[F_B=(1000)(0.020)(9.8)=196\,\mathrm{N}\]

If its weight is \(250\,\mathrm{N}\), the net force is \(196-250=-54\,\mathrm{N}\), so it sinks.

Lab M1 · Buoyancy

The handout asks for buoyant force by two independent measurements on a fully submerged block in water, kerosene, and brine. The block's mass does not disappear in the “loss of mass” method; the spring scale reads a smaller apparent weight because the fluid pushes upward.

Method 1 · displaced fluid
\[V_d=V_f-V_i,\qquad B_1=\rho_{\rm fluid}V_dg\]
For a fully submerged block, \(V_d=V_{\rm block}\). One milliliter equals \(10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}\).
Method 2 · apparent weight
\[B_2=W_{\rm air}-W_{\rm submerged}\]
Both spring-scale readings must be forces in newtons.
\[\text{Percent difference}=\frac{|B_1-B_2|}{(B_1+B_2)/2}\times100\%\]

The printed handout's fluid densities are water \(1000\), kerosene \(820\), and brine \(1030\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\). Use \(g=9.80\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) unless your activity gives another value.

M1 check · Why not use percent error to compare B₁ and B₂?

Neither method is designated an accepted “true” value. Percent difference uses their average as denominator; percent error would require an accepted reference value.

Floating-container extension from the added notebook

For a container floating freely, buoyant force balances its weight. This gives a way to estimate an unknown liquid's density. Use the container's mass, not its weight in newtons, in the numerator:

\[\rho_{\rm fluid}=\frac{m_{\rm container}}{V_{\rm displaced}},\qquad F_B=m_{\rm container}g\]

Notebook example: a 0.0215 kg container displacing 22 mL of water gives:

\[\rho_{\rm exp}=\frac{0.0215\,\mathrm{kg}}{22\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}}\approx977\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\]

Compare with an accepted value using percent error, \(100|\rho_{\rm exp}-\rho_{\rm ref}|/\rho_{\rm ref}\); compare two experimental buoyant-force methods using percent difference (above). These use different denominators.

Concepts behind the fluid formulas

Reference densities, pressure, and limits

Use the density specified in the question. The supplied materials use rounded values of about 1000 kg/m³ for water, 1030 kg/m³ for brine/seawater, 789–790 kg/m³ for ethyl alcohol, and 1.29 kg/m³ for air at reference conditions. Density changes with temperature and composition; these are not universal constants.

Atmospheric pressure acts in every direction. A vacuum cleaner does not literally pull an object: it lowers pressure inside the hose, so higher surrounding air pressure pushes the object toward it. Hydrostatic \(p=p_0+\rho gd\) assumes a fluid at rest, nearly constant density, and vertical depth \(d\). At the same height in one connected static fluid, pressure is equal; deeper points have higher pressure.

IdentifyIs the requested pressure absolute or relative to the surface?CalculateUse \(p_0+\rho gd\) for absolute pressure; use \(\rho gd\) for gauge pressure only when the surface is at atmospheric pressure.CheckThe result must rise with depth and have units of pascals.
Check yourself · Why can a steel ship float?

Its hull encloses air, so its average density based on exterior volume can be less than water's. It displaces enough water for buoyant force to equal its total weight.

Buoyancy when another force acts

The simple sink/float density rule assumes no string or support. A 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm wooden cube (density 700 kg/m³) held fully underwater by a string tied to the bottom has \(V=(0.10)^3=0.001\,\mathrm{m^3}\), \(B=9.8\,\mathrm N\), and \(W=6.86\,\mathrm N\). At rest the downward string force is:

\[T=B-W=9.8-6.86=2.94\,\mathrm N\]

Without the string it rises, then floats with \(V_{\rm sub}/V_{\rm object}=700/1000=0.70\). A freely floating object's buoyancy equals its weight; a fully submerged object need not be in equilibrium.

Module 2 · Thermal Physics

Thermal physics connects temperature, expansion, heat transfer, phase changes, work, and internal energy. Keep temperature, heat, and energy transfer conceptually separate.

1. Temperature scales and thermal expansion

Temperature conversion
\[T_F=\frac95T_C+32\qquad T_K=T_C+273.15\]
A temperature interval has the same numerical size in kelvins and degrees Celsius.
Linear expansion
\[\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\qquad\Delta T=T_f-T_i\]
Use initial length (L_0); assume the object is free to expand.
\[\Delta V=\beta V_0\Delta T\qquad\beta\approx3\alpha\text{ for a small, isotropic solid strain}\]

Typical lab values: steel \(1.2\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm{C}\), brass \(2.0\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Water is anomalous: it is most dense near \(4^\circ\mathrm{C}\), contracting as it warms from 0 to about 4 degrees.

Worked example · 55 m steel pipe from 5°C to 155°C
\[\Delta T=150^\circ\mathrm{C}\qquad\Delta L=(1.1\times10^{-5})(55)(150)\approx0.0908\,\mathrm{m}=9.1\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Lab M2 · Coefficient of linear expansion

The handout's goals are to measure how much brass and steel rods change length, then find each rod's experimental linear expansion coefficient. This is different from merely predicting a length change with an accepted coefficient.

\[\Delta L=L_f-L_0,\qquad \Delta T=T_f-T_i,\qquad \alpha_{\rm exp}=\frac{\Delta L}{L_0\Delta T}\]

\(\alpha\) has units of \(1/{}^\circ\mathrm C\) or \(1/\mathrm K\). Length units cancel if \(L_0\) and \(\Delta L\) use the same unit; keep both in millimeters or both in meters. A 1°C temperature difference equals 1 K difference.

M2 measurement flow

  1. Identify the rod: brass or steel. Record the correct reference coefficient from the handout: brass \(2.0\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm C\), steel \(1.2\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm C\).
  2. Before heating: record initial length \(L_0\) and temperature \(T_i\).
  3. After temperature stabilizes: record final length \(L_f\) and temperature \(T_f\). Read the same length scale consistently.
  4. Find differences: \(\Delta L=L_f-L_0\) and \(\Delta T=T_f-T_i\). Do not insert the final readings directly into the coefficient formula.
  5. Compute \(\alpha_{\rm exp}\): show substitution, units, and scientific notation. Repeat independently for the second metal.
  6. Compare with theory: \(\%\text{ error}=|\alpha_{\rm exp}-\alpha_{\rm theo}|/\alpha_{\rm theo}\times100\%\). Use each metal's own theoretical value.

M2 check · If L₀ doubles but α and ΔT stay fixed, what happens to ΔL?

It doubles because \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\).

M2 check · Which denominator belongs in percent error?

The accepted/theoretical coefficient for that same metal. Dividing by the experimental result would answer a different question.

2. Phase changes and phase diagrams

Sloped heating-curve sections change temperature. Flat sections change phase at constant temperature under fixed pressure.

Temperature change in one phase
\[Q=mc\Delta T\]
Phase change
\[Q=\pm mL\]

Melting, boiling, and sublimation absorb energy; freezing, condensation, and deposition release energy. A phase diagram shows solid, liquid, and gas regions; boundary lines show equilibrium between phases; the triple point contains all three phases; the critical point ends the liquid-gas boundary.

3. Thermal equilibrium, heat, and the first law

The Zeroth Law says that if system C is in thermal equilibrium with A and B, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; an object contains internal energy, not stored heat.

\[\Delta E_{thermal}=W+Q\]

For the module sign convention, energy entering the system is positive: heat entering is (+Q), heat leaving is (-Q), work done on the system is (+W), and work done by the system is (-W).

Worked example · gas energy bookkeeping

Burner adds \(+1500\,\mathrm{J}\); surroundings remove \(400\,\mathrm{J}\), so net heat is \(Q=1100\,\mathrm{J}\). Expansion work by the gas is \(W=-600\,\mathrm{J}\); paddle work on the gas is \(+250\,\mathrm{J}\), so net work is \(W=-350\,\mathrm{J}\).

\[\Delta E_{thermal}=W+Q=-350+1100=750\,\mathrm{J}\]

4. Heat capacity, specific heat, and calorimetry

\[Q=C\Delta T\qquad Q=mc\Delta T\]

Heat capacity (C) belongs to the whole object. Specific heat (c) belongs to the material per unit mass. Use \(mc\Delta T\) only while the substance remains in one phase.

\[\sum Q_i=0\quad\text{for an isolated calorimetry system}\]

Break a multi-stage problem into stages: warm or cool within a phase using \(mc\Delta T\), then add or remove latent heat using (mL). Include every participating object and use signed quantities.

Worked example · melting 2.0 kg of ice at 0°C
\[Q=mL_f=(2.0)(334000)=668000\,\mathrm{J}\]

No \(mc\Delta T\) term is needed because the ice begins at its melting point and changes phase at constant temperature.

5. Heat-transfer mechanisms

Conduction

Energy transfer through matter by microscopic interactions; bodies or regions are in contact.

\[\frac{dQ}{dt}=\frac{kA\Delta T}{L}\]

Convection

Moving fluid carries energy from one region to another. Molecular conduction also occurs within the fluid.

Radiation

Electromagnetic waves transfer energy and do not require matter, so radiation can travel through empty space.

Matter, thermometers, and absolute zero

StateParticle pictureWhat you observe

Temperature indicates thermal state and the direction of spontaneous heat flow; it is not the total thermal energy stored. A thermometer reaches thermal equilibrium with what it measures. A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses expansion; a bimetallic strip bends because its two metals expand differently. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero: \(0\,\mathrm K=-273.15^\circ\mathrm C\). Do not say all internal energy necessarily vanishes there; the gas-thermometer extrapolation is an idealized route to the scale.

Expansion assumptions and thermal stress

Linear expansion \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\) assumes small, approximately uniform temperature change and a material free to expand. If both ends are fixed, the object cannot simply lengthen; stress can develop instead. For ordinary positive \(\alpha\), heating lengthens and cooling shortens. The lecture table lists steel at \(1.1\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm C\) and brass at \(1.9\times10^{-5}/{}^\circ\mathrm C\); the lab handout rounds these to \(1.2\times10^{-5}\) and \(2.0\times10^{-5}\), respectively. Use the coefficient assigned by that question. The approximation \(\beta\approx3\alpha\) is for an isotropic solid under small strain, not a general liquid rule.

Read a phase diagram, not only a heating curve

At a fixed pressure, moving right in the diagram corresponds to heating. Crossing a boundary changes phase. At the triple point, three phases coexist; past the critical point, there is no distinct liquid–gas boundary. The supplied comparison: at about 1 atm, water melts near 0°C and boils near 100°C, whereas carbon dioxide sublimates near −78°C without a stable liquid phase. Phase-change temperatures depend on pressure.

Worked multi-stage example · ice at −20°C in a drink

A 0.25 kg drink treated as water cools from 25°C to 0°C while ice begins at −20°C and ends melted at 0°C. Ignore glass and heat loss. The drink releases \((0.25)(4190)(25)=26187.5\,\mathrm J\). Each kilogram of ice needs \((2090)(20)+334000=375800\,\mathrm{J/kg}\):

\[m_{\rm ice}=\frac{26187.5}{375800}=0.0697\,\mathrm{kg}\approx70\,\mathrm g\]

There are two ice stages—warming and melting—but only one drink stage. Always sketch the temperature path first.

Heat transfer: mechanism and formula limits

ConductionMicroscopic energy transfer through matter. For a uniform slab at steady state, \(\dot Q=kA(T_h-T_c)/L\).ConvectionA moving gas or liquid carries energy; warm fluid may rise because of density differences.RadiationElectromagnetic energy can travel through vacuum; no material medium is required.

In the conduction relation, \(k\) is thermal conductivity in W/(m·K), \(A\) area perpendicular to flow, and \(L\) thickness. Doubling the thickness halves the ideal steady rate; doubling area doubles it. The formula is a magnitude for steady, one-dimensional transfer with roughly constant \(k\), not an automatic model for every changing-temperature situation.

Electricity laboratory concepts

Lab M3 · Van de Graaff generator

A Van de Graaff generator uses a moving belt to transport charge to a hollow metal dome. Charge accumulates on the outer surface, creating very high electric potential and a large electric field.

Chargeaccumulates on the dome
Fieldexerts forces on nearby charges
Observationdepends on material and setup

For the lab, record observations for foil confetti inside the test tube, confetti on top of the dome, a lighted candle, tissue paper, a fluorescent lamp, and human hair. Explain observations using charge movement, electric forces, induction, and the strong electric field. Do not treat an observation alone as a measurement of voltage.

Charge and the three charging methods

Protons carry positive charge; electrons carry equal-magnitude negative charge. A neutral object has equal totals. Gaining electrons makes it negative; losing them makes it positive. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. A charged object can also attract a neutral object by polarizing it.

Friction

Rubbing transfers electrons between materials.

Conduction

Contact lets charge redistribute.

Induction

A nearby charge separates charges without touching. Polarization alone need not change total charge; grounding can leave a net charge.

\[Q=ne,\qquad e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C,\qquad \sum Q_{\rm before}=\sum Q_{\rm after}\]

Use a positive or negative sign for net charge, and \(n\) as the number of elementary charges. Example: gaining \(3.0\times10^9\) electrons gives \(Q=-4.81\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm C\). Charge is transferred, not created. Use these rules to interpret the observation table below; do not copy a possible effect as if it were your measured result.

What the generator changes—and what the observation means

The M3 handout's single objective is to explain effects on different materials. A moving belt transports charge to the metal dome; charge resides on its outer surface, producing a large electric field and potentially a very high potential difference. High voltage alone does not tell you the current through a person or object. Static charge can accumulate, redistribute, and discharge; it is not the same thing as a sustained current in a closed circuit.

How to write an M3 explanation

1 · State the observation“The foil moved away after touching the dome,” not “The foil was negatively charged” unless sign was measured.2 · Name the mechanismPolarization (no contact), conduction/contact charging, or like-charge repulsion.3 · State the limitMovement alone does not reveal the exact charge sign, electric field strength, or voltage.

A neutral object may be attracted by polarization before contact. After it touches and acquires the same kind of charge, repulsion may dominate. This attraction-then-repulsion sequence is useful for confetti, tissue, and a Franklin-bell demonstration mentioned only in the added notebook.

M3 check · Does “attracted to the dome” prove the tissue had the opposite net charge?

No. Neutral tissue can be attracted when its charges polarize, so the closer side has opposite charge even though the whole tissue remains neutral.

M3 check · Two identical isolated spheres have −4 μC and +2 μC. After touching, what is each charge?

Total charge is −2 μC and is conserved. Identical spheres share it equally, so each ends at −1 μC. The equal sharing depends on the spheres being identical and isolated.

M3 check · Why is a glowing fluorescent lamp not a voltage measurement?

Glow is a qualitative effect of the field and tube conditions. It does not by itself specify the dome's potential difference.

M4 · Simple circuit, conductors, and insulators

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point; potential difference is electrical energy change per unit charge; resistance describes opposition to current. A circuit needs an energy source and a complete conducting path. Conductors offer relatively easy movement of charge, while insulators strongly resist it.

\[I=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t},\qquad V=\frac{\Delta U_e}{q},\qquad R=\frac VI\]

Units: \(1\,\mathrm A=1\,\mathrm{C/s}\), \(1\,\mathrm V=1\,\mathrm{J/C}\), and \(1\,\Omega=1\,\mathrm{V/A}\).

How each setup works

Conductivity test: a reliable sequence

  1. Trace the path: find source, lamp, switch, contacts 1 and 2, and return wire. A missing connection anywhere can turn the lamp off.
  2. Try setup 1: with the 1.5 V source, compare lamp-off with switch open and lamp-on with switch closed. Draw both states.
  3. Prepare setup 2: use the specified 12 V source and rated lamp. Put the 0/1 switch at 0 while placing the sample across contacts 1 and 2. Follow the instructor's equipment procedure; do not bypass the lamp or short the supply.
  4. Test a known conductor first: if the lamp does not light, check source, switch, lamp, and contact quality before testing unknown pieces.
  5. Change one thing: swap only the sample. Record its name, condition, and whether the lamp is bright, dim, or off.
  6. Interpret: a lit lamp supports sufficient conductivity in this setup. An unlit lamp alone does not prove the sample is an insulator.

Energy story and useful quantities

BatteryChemical energy maintains a potential difference.CurrentCharge per time: \(I=\Delta Q/\Delta t\). 12 C in 4 s gives 3 A.VoltageEnergy per charge: \(V=\Delta U/q\). 24 J per 2 C gives 12 V.

The lamp changes electrical energy into light and thermal energy. Charge is conserved around a steady series loop; the lamp does not “use up” current. In metal wires electrons drift from negative to positive, whereas conventional current points positive to negative.

Notebook extension: \(V=IR\) and \(P=VI\) support calculations but are not formulas displayed in the M4 slides. At the lamp's rated 12 V and 3 W, \(I=P/V=0.25\,\mathrm A\) and operating \(R=V/I\approx48\,\Omega\). That is a rated operating value, not a constant cold-bulb resistance.

M4 practice · try before opening

1 · The switch is open and the lamp is off. Is the lamp faulty?

Not necessarily. The open switch intentionally breaks the path; close it in the correct setup before diagnosing the lamp.

2 · A metal piece gives no light. What else might be wrong?

The source, switch, lamp, or either contact may have failed; a coating or oxide can also block good contact. Test a known conductor before judging the piece.

3 · What symbols belong in a battery–lamp–closed-switch loop?

Long/short battery plates with polarity, a lamp circle, joined switch contacts, and continuous wire lines. Physical cable shape does not matter; connectivity does.

4 · Six coulombs pass a point in three seconds. Find current.

\(I=Q/t=6/3=2\,\mathrm A\). This comes directly from the definition of current.

Check yourself · Friction, conduction, or induction?

Rubbing transfers electrons by friction. Touching a charged body allows charge transfer by conduction. Bringing one near without contact causes charge separation by induction; grounding is needed if you want a formerly neutral object to retain net charge after the nearby body is removed.

Check yourself · Why do hair strands spread on the dome?

The strands acquire charge of the same sign and repel. The outward spreading is not evidence that hair is “magnetic.”

1 · Hydrostatic pressure on Mars

The sheet gives a 0.500 km freshwater ocean and \(g_{\rm Mars}=3.71\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). Convert depth to 500 m:

\[p_g=\rho gd=(1000)(3.71)(500)=1.855\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\]

For the same gauge pressure in Earth's seawater, assume \(\rho_{\rm sea}=1030\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(g=9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\):

\[d=\frac{1.855\times10^6}{(1030)(9.8)}\approx184\,\mathrm m\]
2 · Pascal's principle with a student and an elephant

Part A: a 70 kg student balances a 1200 kg elephant on a 2.0 m diameter piston. Equal pressures give:

\[D_s=2.0\sqrt{70/1200}\approx0.483\,\mathrm m\]

Part B: after the second student joins, the small piston drops 0.35 m and the large piston rises \(0.35(70/1200)=0.0204\,\mathrm m\). The new height difference is 0.3704 m. Including that oil head, the added mass is:

\[m_2=\rho_{\rm oil}A_s(0.3704),\qquad A_s=\pi(1.0)^2(70/1200)\approx0.1833\,\mathrm{m^2}\]

The sheet does not specify oil density, so the numerical mass is not unique. If \(\rho_{\rm oil}=900\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\) is assumed, \(m_2\approx61\,\mathrm{kg}\). Confirm the class assumption.

3 · Density, buoyancy, and Archimedes' principle

Air, water, and unknown-liquid tensions are 39.2 N, 28.4 N, and 18.6 N. Thus \(B_w=10.8\,\mathrm N\) and \(B_u=20.6\,\mathrm N\). For the same submerged volume:

\[\rho_u=\rho_w\frac{B_u}{B_w}=1000\frac{20.6}{10.8}\approx1.91\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\]
4 · Thermal expansion of the Burj Khalifa

Given \(L_0=828\,\mathrm m\) and \(\Delta L=36\,\mathrm{cm}=0.36\,\mathrm m\), using the lab's steel coefficient \(\alpha=1.2\times10^{-5}\,{}^\circ\mathrm C^{-1}\):

\[\Delta T=\frac{0.36}{(1.2\times10^{-5})(828)}\approx36.2^\circ\mathrm C\]

A different tabulated steel coefficient gives a different answer; state yours.

5 · Calorimetry with aluminum, copper, and ethyl alcohol

Given 10 g aluminum at 200°C, 20 g copper, 50 cm³ ethanol at 15°C, and final 25°C. The module table gives \(c_{Al}=900\), \(c_{Cu}=385\), and \(c_{eth}=2400\,\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}\). With \(\rho_{eth}=789\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), ethanol mass is 0.03945 kg. Heat balance:

\[(0.010)(900)(25-200)+(0.020)(385)(25-T_{Cu,i})+(0.03945)(2400)(25-15)=0\]

Solving gives \(T_{Cu,i}\approx-56.6^\circ\mathrm C\). This idealized result ignores heat loss and container heat capacity.

Six-question retrieval check

Answer aloud or on paper before opening each explanation.

1 · Two holes at different depths in the same tank: which has greater pressure?

The deeper hole. With the same surface pressure and fluid, \(p=p_0+\rho gd\) rises with vertical depth.

2 · Why does the wider hydraulic piston move less?

The displaced fluid volume must match: \(A_1d_1=A_2d_2\). A larger area requires a smaller distance for the same volume.

3 · A pure substance is melting at fixed pressure while heated. Does its temperature rise?

Not until melting finishes. During the equilibrium phase change, supplied energy changes phase; model it with \(Q=mL_f\), not \(mc\Delta T\).

4 · What is the difference between heat capacity and specific heat?

Heat capacity \(C\), in J/K, belongs to the whole object. Mass-specific heat \(c\), in J/(kg·K), belongs to the material, with \(C=mc\) when the approximation applies.

5 · Can a neutral object be attracted to a charged one?

Yes. A nearby charge can polarize it so opposite charge is closer; attraction does not prove the neutral object gained net charge.

6 · A test bulb stays off. Have you proved the sample is an insulator?

No. The sample might resist current, but the source, switch, bulb, or contacts might also have broken the path. Check the rest of the circuit first.
